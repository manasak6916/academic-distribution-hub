# Unlocking Materials Discovery: A Comprehensive Guide to High-Throughput Experimental Databases

## Summary
High-Throughput Experimental Materials (HTEM) Databases are transforming materials science by providing large-scale, publicly accessible experimental data, which is crucial for training machine learning (ML) models. These databases, such as the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's (NREL) HTEM-DB, address the 'data gap' between abundant computational predictions and scarce experimental results. They are built on a sophisticated Research Data Infrastructure (RDI) that automates the collection, processing, and storage of data from laboratory instruments. The data includes synthesis conditions, chemical composition, crystal structure, and various functional properties for materials like inorganic thin-films. By standardizing and centralizing this information, HTEM databases enable researchers to discover complex process-structure-property relationships. Recent advancements, like a fully automated system from NIMS, have accelerated data generation by 200-fold, demonstrating the immense potential of these resources to accelerate materials-driven innovation in energy, computing, and drug development.

## Extended Summary
High-Throughput Experimental Materials (HTEM) Databases represent a fundamental shift in materials research, designed to accelerate innovation by systematically aggregating and disseminating large volumes of experimental data. These resources address the critical 'data gap' in materials science, where the scarcity of diverse, high-quality experimental datasets has hindered the application of machine learning (ML). Unlike computational databases that store predicted properties, HTEM databases like the one developed at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) contain empirical data from combinatorial experiments on materials such as inorganic thin-films. As of 2018, NREL's HTEM-DB housed over 140,000 sample entries, including synthesis conditions, structural data from X-ray diffraction, chemical composition, and optoelectronic properties.

The foundation of these databases is a robust Research Data Infrastructure (RDI), an automated system that manages the entire data lifecycle. The RDI features components like data harvesters that monitor instruments, a central Data Warehouse, a Laboratory Metadata Collector (LMC) to capture experimental context, and Extract, Transform, Load (ETL) scripts to structure the data. This infrastructure overcomes challenges like data heterogeneity and 'dark data' (unpublished results) by creating standardized, ML-ready datasets. Access is provided through web interfaces for exploration and APIs for programmatic analysis. The impact is significant, as demonstrated by an automated system from Japan's National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS) that generated a comprehensive dataset in 13 days, a task that would conventionally take over seven years, marking a 200-fold acceleration in data generation.
