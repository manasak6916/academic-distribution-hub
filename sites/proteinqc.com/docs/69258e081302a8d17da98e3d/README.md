# Unfolded Protein Response (UPR): From ER Stress Mechanisms to Therapeutic Targeting in Disease

## Summary
The Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) is essential for cellular protein synthesis and folding. When protein folding demand exceeds capacity, ER stress occurs, triggering the Unfolded Protein Response (UPR) to restore proteostasis. This comprehensive review details the molecular mechanisms of the three primary UPR sensors: PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6. Initially, the UPR acts as an adaptive, pro-survival mechanism by attenuating global protein translation, upregulating ER chaperones, and enhancing ER-associated degradation. However, under severe or prolonged stress, the UPR transitions to a pro-apoptotic signaling cascade, leading to cell death. The article explores the pathophysiological significance of UPR dysregulation in various conditions, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, metabolic diseases, and immune dysregulation. Furthermore, it provides extensive experimental protocols for inducing and monitoring ER stress, troubleshooting common analytical pitfalls, and validating UPR activation. Finally, the review highlights the emerging potential of UPR-targeted therapeutics, discussing preclinical inhibitors and context-dependent modulation strategies for novel disease treatments.

## Extended Summary
The Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) plays a critical role in the synthesis, folding, and modification of secretory and transmembrane proteins. Disruptions to ER homeostasis lead to the accumulation of misfolded proteins, a condition known as ER stress. To mitigate this stress, eukaryotic cells activate the Unfolded Protein Response (UPR), an integrated signaling network orchestrated by three ER-transmembrane sensors: PERK, IRE1α, and ATF6. Under normal conditions, these sensors are kept inactive by the chaperone protein BiP. Upon ER stress, BiP dissociates, allowing the sensors to activate distinct signaling cascades. The PERK pathway phosphorylates eIF2α to globally attenuate translation while selectively promoting ATF4 expression. The IRE1α pathway splices XBP1 mRNA to produce a potent transcription factor that enhances ER folding capacity. The ATF6 pathway involves transit to the Golgi apparatus for proteolytic cleavage, releasing an active transcription factor.

The UPR exhibits a dual nature: it initially drives adaptive, pro-survival responses to restore cellular homeostasis but can pivot to activate pro-apoptotic signaling, primarily through CHOP and JNK pathways, under conditions of severe or irremediable stress. This delicate balance determines cell fate and is heavily implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases. In cancer, tumor cells exploit the adaptive UPR to survive hostile microenvironments and resist therapies. In neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, chronic ER stress and maladaptive UPR signaling drive neuronal loss. Additionally, the UPR regulates immune cell function and contributes to metabolic syndromes.

Given its central role in disease, the UPR represents a highly promising therapeutic target. Researchers are actively developing pharmacological modulators, such as IRE1α RNase inhibitors and PERK kinase inhibitors, to selectively disrupt pro-survival signaling in tumors or prevent apoptosis in neurodegeneration. This review also provides a robust technical framework for investigating the UPR, detailing standardized in vitro and in vivo experimental protocols, essential research reagents like tunicamycin and thapsigargin, and advanced multi-omics methodologies for resolving cell-type-specific UPR heterogeneity. By navigating the complexities of UPR signaling, researchers can better understand its systemic effects and advance the development of precision interventions.
