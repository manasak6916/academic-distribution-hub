# Biomolecular Condensates and Protein Aggregates in Human Disease: From Basic Mechanisms to Therapeutic Targeting

## Summary
This comprehensive article explores the dual roles of biomolecular condensates and protein aggregates in physiological processes and human disease pathogenesis. Biomolecular condensates are membraneless compartments formed via liquid-liquid phase separation that organize cellular biochemistry. While essential for normal cellular function, these dynamic, liquid-like structures can undergo aberrant phase transitions into solid-like, pathological aggregates. This liquid-to-solid transition is a hallmark of numerous neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, as well as cancer. The text details the biophysical principles driving condensate formation, particularly the role of intrinsically disordered proteins and multivalent interactions. Furthermore, it outlines advanced research methodologies, including quantitative imaging, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching, and AI-driven predictive tools, used to characterize these assemblies. Finally, the article highlights emerging therapeutic strategies, specifically focusing on condensate-modifying drugs such as dissolvers, inducers, localizers, and morphers, which offer novel avenues for targeting previously undruggable proteins in complex diseases.

## Extended Summary
This extensive review provides a detailed examination of biomolecular condensates and protein aggregates, emphasizing their fundamental biophysical principles, role in disease pathogenesis, and potential as therapeutic targets. Biomolecular condensates are dynamic, membraneless cellular compartments formed through liquid-liquid phase separation. They compartmentalize specific proteins and nucleic acids to regulate essential cellular processes like gene expression and stress response. The formation of these condensates is largely driven by multivalent interactions involving intrinsically disordered proteins and specific RNA molecules.

Under normal physiological conditions, cellular quality control mechanisms, including molecular chaperones and post-translational modifications, maintain the dynamic, liquid-like state of these condensates. However, aging, genetic mutations, and environmental stressors such as hypoxia can disrupt this delicate homeostasis. This dysregulation frequently triggers an aberrant liquid-to-solid phase transition, causing functional condensates to mature into irreversible, toxic protein aggregates. This pathological transition is a unifying mechanism underlying major neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's disease involving amyloid-beta and tau, Parkinson's disease involving alpha-synuclein, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis involving TDP-43 and FUS. Furthermore, dysregulated condensates are increasingly implicated in cancer, where oncogenic fusion proteins create aberrant condensates that drive tumorigenesis and metabolic adaptation.

To investigate these phenomena, researchers employ a multidisciplinary toolkit. Advanced methodologies include Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching to assess condensate dynamics, 1,6-hexanediol sensitivity assays to probe hydrophobic interactions, and AI-driven microscopy paired with CRISPR-Cas systems for real-time tracking and genetic manipulation. In vitro reconstitution, cellular models, and organoids provide complementary platforms for validating disease mechanisms.

Recognizing the critical role of condensates in disease has catalyzed a new frontier in drug discovery: condensate-modifying drugs. These therapeutics are classified into four phenotypic categories: dissolvers, inducers, localizers, and morphers, based on their ability to modulate condensate formation, dissolution, or material properties. By targeting the physical state transitions of these assemblies, condensate-modifying drugs offer a transformative approach to treating complex protein aggregation diseases and targeting proteins historically deemed undruggable.
