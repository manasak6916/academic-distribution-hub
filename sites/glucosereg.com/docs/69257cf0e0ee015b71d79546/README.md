# Thyroid Hormones T3 and T4 as Central Regulators of Basal Metabolic Rate: Molecular Mechanisms, Metabolic Integration, and Therapeutic Potential

## Summary
Thyroid hormones, specifically thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), serve as central regulators of mammalian basal metabolic rate (BMR), energy expenditure, and thermogenesis. The hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis tightly controls hormone synthesis, while peripheral deiodinase enzymes and specific cellular transporters intricately regulate local intracellular T3 availability. Thyroid hormones govern metabolism through both canonical genomic signaling, mediated by nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (TR α and TR β), and rapid non-genomic pathways involving cell surface receptors like integrin αvβ3. Dysregulation of these pathways contributes significantly to metabolic disorders, including obesity, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome. Consequently, advancing our understanding of these molecular mechanisms has spurred the development of novel therapeutic interventions. Promising strategies include TR β-selective agonists, such as resmetirom, which target hepatic lipid metabolism while minimizing adverse cardiovascular effects associated with TR α activation. Endogenous metabolites like 3,5-T2 also offer potential metabolic benefits. This comprehensive synthesis highlights the critical role of thyroid hormones in metabolic physiology and their emerging potential as targeted therapeutics for metabolic diseases.

## Extended Summary
Thyroid hormones (THs), primarily thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), function as the master regulators of mammalian basal metabolic rate (BMR) and systemic energy homeostasis. The hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis maintains systemic TH levels through a hierarchical neuroendocrine feedback loop. However, the ultimate metabolic impact of TH is dictated at the cellular level by a sophisticated prereceptor control system involving specific plasma membrane transporters (such as MCT8 and MCT10) and deiodinase enzymes (DIO1, DIO2, and DIO3). These components meticulously regulate the intracellular availability of active T3, enabling tissues to customize their metabolic responses independently of circulating hormone levels.

At the molecular level, THs exert their influence through both genomic and non-genomic mechanisms. Genomic signaling involves T3 binding to nuclear thyroid hormone receptors (TR α and TR β), which heterodimerize with retinoid X receptors (RXRs) to modulate the transcription of genes critical for mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, and substrate cycling. Concurrently, non-genomic pathways are activated via cell surface receptors, such as integrin αvβ3, initiating rapid intracellular signaling cascades that further fine-tune cellular metabolism and energy expenditure.

Dysfunction within this regulatory network precipitates profound metabolic consequences. Hypothyroidism reduces BMR and promotes weight gain and dyslipidemia by downregulating lipid catabolism and clearance pathways. Conversely, hyperthyroidism induces a paradoxical catabolic state characterized by increased energy expenditure coupled with the depletion of metabolic reserves. Furthermore, even subclinical thyroid dysfunction is strongly associated with metabolic syndrome, highlighting the exquisite sensitivity of metabolic homeostasis to TH variations.

Recognizing the limitations of conventional levothyroxine monotherapy, which often fails to fully resolve metabolic symptoms in a subset of patients, contemporary research has pivoted toward targeted therapeutic interventions. A primary focus is the development of TR β-selective agonists, such as resmetirom and VK2809. These compounds aim to harness the beneficial lipid-lowering and metabolic effects of TH action in the liver while circumventing the deleterious cardiovascular and skeletal side effects mediated by TR α. Additionally, endogenous TH metabolites like 3,5-T2 are being investigated for their potent mitochondrial and anti-steatotic properties. Ultimately, integrating a precise understanding of TH signaling with advanced metabolic phenotyping holds significant promise for developing personalized, highly effective treatments for obesity, dyslipidemia, and related metabolic disorders.
